# The Organization and the Individual in the AI Era

> **Languages:** [English](ai-era-organization-individual-en.md) (primary) · [中文](ai-era-organization-individual.md)
> Created 2026-07-02

**Core thesis**: The productivity structure of the AI era is undergoing a fundamental shift—organizations turning from control-oriented to collaboration-oriented, individuals turning from execution to oversight and decision-making. The direction of organizational evolution is the **liquid organization**: no fixed positions or departments within the organization; people and AI are dynamically grouped by an orchestrator according to the task. The individual's positioning returns to the power-law tail—AI covers execution in the normal-distribution range, while humans monopolize judgment and review. The dividend cannot be cashed out in old companies: "enterprise AI transformation" is a false proposition; two walls, incentive and structure, block the way, and renewal happens only at the level of the population—the AI-native form can only grow from zero, or be grafted on through acquisition.

---

## The Fundamental Shift in the Productivity Structure

Organizations turn from control-oriented to collaboration-oriented.

**The applicable premises of the control-oriented organization**. The control-oriented organization is not a design failure; it is the solution adapted to the production conditions it fit. It is efficient on two premises: first, labor intensity—output mainly comes from stacking manpower, and the manager's duty is to organize large amounts of manpower into reliable processes; second, the matters are numerous and complex but standardized—the tasks themselves are trivial and repetitive, do not depend on the executor's creativity, and only need to be executed without error. On these two premises, layer-by-layer instruction is the optimal coordination method: cut the complex business into standard actions; each action need not understand the whole, only obey scheduling. The factory assembly line, the large call center, and chain operation are all victories of this form.

**The two premises weaken simultaneously in the AI era**. Standardized matters no longer need manpower stacking—AI takes on a large number of standardized processes at extremely low cost, and the scope it takes on is still expanding; more critically, AI's core advantage is precisely handling **non-standardized processes**: understanding context, judging intent, and making reasonable dispositions where the rules do not cover. This means the constraint that "a task must be cut fine and standardized before it can be handed to manpower" has disappeared—a vague business goal can be handed directly to AI, and the intermediate standardized decomposition is no longer necessary. Labor intensity and transaction standardization—the two pillars on which the control-oriented organization rests—are pulled out at the same time.

**The inevitability of the turn to collaboration**. The liberated manpower is not unemployment but upward movement—turning to oversight and decision-making. And the qualities oversight and decision-making require are completely different from execution: execution can be driven by instruction (do this, by this standard), while oversight and decision-making rely on **initiative and responsibility**—finding problems, being accountable for results, and making judgments where there is no explicit instruction. These qualities cannot be driven by instruction; they can only be aroused by ownership. The control-oriented management style fails at this: you cannot order a person to be "proactive"; the endpoint of instruction-driven management is the execution layer. So the management style itself is forced to change—from control to collaboration, giving the individual greater autonomous space to carry judgment and responsibility.

**The individual side shifts in step**: from executor to overseer and decision-maker—AI handles execution, and the human appears at the judgment points and oversight positions (the mechanism of this shift is unfolded later in the liquid organization's division-of-labor structure and the change in assessment).

## Two Walls: Why Old Companies Cannot Turn

The same AI, in an individual's hands, boosts efficiency a hundredfold, while in an organization's hands only two or three tenths are left—this gap is not a tool problem but two structural walls. The contrast can be checked against public figures: Google reported that AI raised engineering speed by about 10% (measured as engineering hours saved per week); Meta reported that since early 2025 per-capita engineering output has risen 30%, and heavy users 80% year over year—both are organizational figures, with a denominator different from the individual figure of "one person, on a subscription, doing the work of a team," so they cannot be directly subtracted, but the order-of-magnitude gap is real. The two walls each explain half of this gap.

### The Incentive Wall: The Gains from Speed Do Not Belong to You

Under the employment system, a person who uses AI to speed up tenfold does not get tenfold income, nor tenfold leisure—the most likely outcome is that the capable do more work, and the boss hands tenfold more work back for the extra capacity. So the rational choice is to use it quietly and say little: hand in only onefold of the difference on tenfold efficiency, and keep the rest for slacking off, side gigs, and leaving early. This is not speculation—a 2025 global survey by KPMG and the University of Melbourne (47 countries, 48,000 people) found that 57% of employees conceal their AI use and package AI output as their own results.

Concealment is individually rational, but summed up it is the organization's collective irrationality: the dividend is intercepted by individuals, and the organization's books reflect only that onefold and a bit. This logic has a historical prototype for its solution—the household contract responsibility system. Justin Yifu Lin estimated that about 42–46% of China's agricultural output growth from 1978 to 1984 can be attributed to the contract reform. What it did was not "make the production team better at managing people," but dismantle the production unit from the production team back to a single household, making people the residual claimants of their own labor ("hand over enough to the state, leave enough for the collective, and the rest is your own"). In other words, this is not a collective successfully transforming itself, but the collective dismantling itself and reducing the incentive problem back to the ownership problem. In the liquid organization, "instant settlement, contribution-index tokens, private-key work badges" is precisely meant to rebuild this residual claim right inside the organization, so that the gains from speed can land in the individual's hands—otherwise the incentive wall will not open the door for any AI-native organization.

### The Structural Wall: Serial Responsibility Deadlocks Parallel Speedup

Even if incentives are sorted out and everyone is willing to work hard, there is a second wall: coordination cost.

Amdahl's law gives the cleanest formulation: how much faster a system can go depends on how large the proportion that can be accelerated is. Even if the parallelizable part is accelerated to infinity, the overall ceiling is still deadlocked by the serial proportion. Suppose R&D (the parallelizable part) speeds up 100-fold, but an organization spends 90% of its time on the serial chain of review, approval, reporting, and handoff—then the whole is only a bit over onefold faster, landing right near the figure Google reported.

The key is that what really jams the serial chain is not information but responsibility. Meeting minutes, review comments, and weekly reports can all be written by AI; but "who decides, who signs, and who takes the blame when something goes wrong"—these three things AI cannot replace, and the organization will not let it. Information is what AI can compress; responsibility is the anchor the organization must keep in the person—which exactly explains why "give every employee an AI subscription" does not speed things up: it accelerates the parallelizable information processing, but cannot move the non-transferable serial segment of responsibility. The theory of constraints puts it more bluntly: speeding up a non-bottleneck only piles up inventory in front of the bottleneck. Faros's R&D telemetry is exactly this form—high-AI-adoption teams merging 98% more PRs also saw PR review time rise 91%: code is written lightning-fast, and it all jams on the serial segment of human review (responsibility gatekeeping).

Bureaucracy is the physical cause of this wall. The channel model of The Mythical Man-Month (n people have n(n-1)/2 communication paths between them, see "The True Cause of Position-Boundary Dissolution" later) shows that communication cost grows with the square of the number of people; the point of bureaucracy's existence is to compress this mesh into a tree (each layer reports only to its superior), reducing coordination complexity from O(n²) to about O(n log n), at the cost of turning communication that could have been parallel into a serial approval chain. The taller the tree, the longer the chain, and the larger Amdahl's serial proportion. West adds macro-level evidence in Scale: companies scale like organisms rather than cities, sublinearly—tenfold the people does not tenfold the output. The structural wall thus stands firm: the old company's organizational topology is itself a device that lowers parallelism, and AI cannot get in to parallelize.

### The Rhyme of History: Electrification and the Drive Shaft

Economic historian Paul David's The Dynamo and the Computer records an almost identical prehistory. Electricity was commercialized in the 1880s, but by the turn of the century electric motors accounted for less than 5% of mechanical power in American factories; early factories merely replaced the steam engine with a single large motor, still driving the central drive shaft that ran through the plant, and the effect was disappointing. Only when unit drive—from group drive to one motor per machine—became widespread did American manufacturing productivity truly take off—about forty years after electricity's commercialization. David's explanation is plain: the old plant could still be used, and tearing it down to rebuild was not worth it, which held back adoption of the new paradigm.

Today, "give every employee an AI subscription" is replacing the steam engine with a motor in place, without moving the drive shaft. Today's drive shaft is the bureaucratic organization and the approval chain (the non-transferable serial segment of responsibility); today's plant is the organization itself. The ones who truly reaped the electrification dividend were the new factories that rearranged the plant around unit drive—corresponding to today, the AI-native organizations built from zero on a liquid topology, rather than old companies that bolt AI onto an old topology.

### Metabolism at the Level of the Population

Does old companies not getting the dividend mean they will die out? West's team counted more than twenty thousand North American listed companies from 1950 to 2009: the typical half-life is about ten years, and the mortality rate is independent of company age (no aging effect). The most common "way of dying" is being acquired. Individual companies come and go, but the whole economy keeps renewing itself—**metabolism happens at the level of the population, not the individual**. No matter how many subscriptions an old company issues to its employees, it cannot become AI-native; it can only be replaced: either a new species grows from zero (frontier labs like OpenAI and Anthropic are closest to the new form, and all grew from zero, not transformed out of some old company), or the old company acquires the new plant and keeps it operating independently (Google bought DeepMind in 2014 and ran it as an independent unit for nearly a decade before merging it in—equivalent to buying a new plant rather than renovating the old one).

This does not conflict with this article's liquid-organization thesis but is its realistic-constraint side: the liquid organization describes what the new species should look like, and the two walls explain why the old species cannot grow over. Renewal depends on generational replacement, not individual transformation.

## The Organization: From Bureaucracy to the Liquid Organization

### The Liquid Organization: Temporary Groupings Under AI Orchestration

The foundation of productivity has changed, and the organizational form changes with it. The shift in the previous section—AI takes over execution (including non-standardized processes), humans turn to oversight and decision-making—lands on organizational structure with the direct conclusion: fixed positions and departments lose their reason to exist. Bureaucracy and functional division solved the problem of "how to organize large amounts of manpower into standardized processes," and this problem is disappearing; on the new foundation, the organization must solve the reverse problem: **how to efficiently combine the people who carry judgment and responsibility with AI's execution capability**.

The liquid organization (Liquid Organization) is the solution to this problem: AI is no longer merely a tool invoked by human employees, but turns directly into the organization's orchestrator, routing switch, and dynamic manager—it thoroughly shatters the traditional org chart, treating people and AI as productivity cells that can be recombined on demand. Within the organization there are no fixed positions, fixed departments, or rigid processes—the only constant is that the AI orchestrator, based on business goals emerging in real time, dynamically matches people and AI into temporary groupings; the task is dissolved the moment it ends, the state is frozen and sedimented, and the organization flows like water through the gaps in the business.

The matching mechanism of groupings: the orchestrator continuously observes members' collaboration and output, dynamically maintaining a map of members' capabilities and experience (members need not manually fill out résumés or skill lists); when a task comes in, it is split by content, matched to the currently most competent and idle members, and pushed directly into place—matching turns from "meeting to assign" into infrastructure. The human oversight-and-decision-making role is embedded in this: human members within a grouping carry judgment and gatekeeping, AI carries execution; how the formation and dissolution of groupings is constrained by humans is covered in the life-cycle protocol below.

This "push directly into place" is the key that distinguishes the liquid organization from ant-colony-style bureaucracy. Ant colonies coordinate by slowly diffusing pheromones: individuals leave chemical traces, other individuals follow them, and the signal gradually diffuses along the ground, slow and prone to distortion—this is exactly the organizational version of bureaucracy relaying messages level by level along the reporting chain. Vertebrates switched to a different body design: the lungs actively inhale, and the heart and blood vessels pump oxygen under pressure to every cell; organs are connected in real time by blood and nerves, not by diffusion. The liquid organization's orchestrator is this circulatory system—context, tasks, and validation do not diffuse along the hierarchy, but are actively pumped under pressure into the hands of those who should have them. With equal internal complexity (the division of labor is no less than an ant colony's), it can be both large and fast at once, because coordination goes by pumping, not diffusion.

The driving mode of operation is **event-driven, task-seeks-person**—exactly the opposite of the traditional organization's "person-seeks-task." In traditional organizations, people take their places first (position, department), and once an event occurs it flows up along the reporting chain, waiting for a manager to match the person with the task; what flows is information, while people sit still. In the liquid organization, the event is the prime mover: a business event occurs (a bug appears, an order comes in, a user complains), the orchestrator immediately parses the event's type and scale, and actively pushes the task to the most matching member—what flows is the task, while people sit still at their judgment positions. People are awakened by events, not scheduled by a shift roster; between two events a person is in a low-power state (Scale-to-Zero), and enters the working state as soon as the event arrives. The five lines of the main scenario and the late-night hack of the emergency scenario are both concretizations of this mode: every line begins with an external event and ends with the grouping's dissolution—event arises, grouping gathers; event dies, grouping disperses.

Event-driven is not a new idea—alerting systems and workflow engines have long worked this way—but it used to befit only loose organizations. The bottleneck was policy maintenance: writing "what event, routed to whom, escalated by what rule" into the rules of a policy engine; once the business changes, the rules rot, maintenance cost grows superlinearly with the number of rules, and the policy engine finally degenerates into a legacy system no one dares touch. This is the real reason event-driven does not catch on in traditional organizations; it is not that the idea does not hold. What AI changes is this cost structure: the translation between the natural-language expression and the code expression of a policy is handled by AI, and when the business changes, it suffices to state the new rule clearly in words—policy maintenance degrades from an engineering act to a conversational act. Further, the orchestrator itself can be the policy engine: it continuously observes routing outcomes in the event stream (which matches were right, which were manually rerouted) and sediments the corrections back into itself—the policy is no longer a static asset to be maintained, but a capability that evolves with use. The cost is that the policy is no longer fully enumerable or pre-auditable; this is backstopped by the human Gate in the grouping life-cycle protocol: routing outcomes are auditable, misroutes are correctable, and the correction itself becomes nourishment for policy evolution.

This operation relies on three basic properties for support:

- **Individual sovereignty and identity binding (private-key work badge)**: the organization does not set up a centrally controlled power to "ban" accounts. Whether a human member or an AI agent, its identity is determined by the cryptographic private key it holds (the public key is the unique ID), and the member's assets, contribution score, and reputation within the organization are bound to the private key. Managerially, this gives members psychological safety and creative sovereignty—contributions belong to oneself, not to the platform.
- **Low power when idle (Scale-to-Zero)**: in traditional management, a project team still occupies a great deal of administrative overhead, weekly-reporting cost, and communication bandwidth when idle. The liquid organization pursues "nothingness when idle": after the event-driven hack is completed, the temporary squad dissolves at the system level, and state information is frozen and sedimented—administrative drag and energy occupation approach zero, quietly awaiting the dynamic awakening of the next event.
- **Encapsulation toward the inside (Encapsulation)**: it must be made clear that the liquid organization is not a plaza fully open to the public (like a borderless open-source community). It is an inward-facing symbiont with commercial boundaries—from the outside it looks like a static wall with clear boundaries, while internally it is a transparent mesh of high concurrency and seamless human-machine collaboration. It trades an extremely low external trust overhead for internal trust and high efficiency. The strength of the boundary is asymmetric: clear externally (the outside sees only one boundary, whose contact surface is held by AI), weaker internally—internally there is almost no boundary, with highly fluid human-machine collaboration, and the only hard boundary lies between the organization and the outside world.

### Main Scenario: The Everyday Operation of the AI-Managed Boundary

The liquid organization's main scenario is everyday operation—the organization's boundary is fluid: borderless and highly fluid internally, with the contact surface to the outside managed by AI and supervised by humans, and the interaction content triggering internal processes. Several parallel lines can show the depth and breadth of scheduling collaboration:

- **External-interaction line (AI-managed boundary, stakeholders can step in at any time)**: external users converse with AI, and the conversation content is synced to stakeholders in real time—in an organization that sells goods the stakeholder is customer service; in one that buys goods the stakeholder is procurement. Day-to-day conversations are handled directly by AI, with human oversight offline; as soon as a context arises that AI is unsure of, the stakeholder can step in at any time and take over.
- **Business-process trigger line (interaction turns into internal process)**: a problem is recognized in the conversation, and different processes are launched by type—if out of stock, contact procurement to restock; if there is an order, go through logistics to ship. The latter is often already automated by existing business systems, which gives rise to a new collaboration problem: how AI connects with existing systems. In the liquid organization this is one of the orchestrator's duties—it is not only a router between people and AI within the organization, but also the adaptation layer between internal processes and existing business systems.
- **Product-improvement line (conversation sedimentation → aggregation → discussion and assignment)**: product/software improvement points that surface in conversations are continuously aggregated by AI and sent to the product manager and stakeholders; after a discussion yields a result, AI decomposes the conclusion into tasks and assigns them to developers; the developers make a plan, AI assists development and submits a PR, and the developers accept it. Along the whole line, what AI carries is the mechanical part of information hauling, aggregation, and decomposition, while judgment (whether to change, how to change, acceptance) stays in human hands.
- **Compliance-and-risk line (escalation decisions)**: when complaints, disputes, or regulatory-sensitive topics appear in a conversation, AI identifies the risk level—above the threshold, hand off to legal/compliance staff; low-risk, soothe by template and record. This line produces no business action, only escalation decisions.
- **Proactive-outreach line (reverse information flow)**: it does not only passively take conversations—AI proactively reaches out to the outside based on internal signals (order delays, version upgrades, service expiry), initiating follow-ups or notifications. The previous lines are all external information flowing in; this one is internal signals flowing out—the two-way nature of the boundary.

AI carries the mechanical part of orchestration and execution, and humans appear only at the judgment points and oversight positions—this is the division-of-labor structure shared by these lines.

### Emergency Scenario: The Five-Minute Hack of a Late-Night Vulnerability

An emergency is an extreme test of this mechanism. At two in the morning, the core product triggers an unknown high-severity security vulnerability. There is no hierarchical reporting—none of the redundant relay of "the on-call ops person phones the supervisor, the supervisor reports to the CTO, the CTO convenes an emergency meeting, cross-departmental coordination pulls in people." The orchestrator retrieves that the vulnerability's characteristics are highly correlated with a piece of low-level refactoring code from three months ago, and that the expert responsible for that code at the time is on vacation; at the same time there happens to be an AI patch agent in the system with rich experience handling this kind of vulnerability. The orchestrator immediately forms a temporary grouping: AI is sufficient to carry the fix execution (generating a draft, running tests); the expert is awakened not because he holds technical capability that AI lacks, but because the judgment responsibility for this crisis needs a human to carry it—this is exactly the shape of the new division-of-labor structure under extreme conditions: execution is carried entirely by AI, and the human appears only at the responsibility and gatekeeping positions. The expert receives the highest-level awakening; the instant he opens the terminal, the map has already presented on the screen the relevant code-change context, the prerequisite test conditions, and the AI's pre-generated fix draft. Human and AI run in parallel on the same work surface—AI tests the logic at machine speed, the human does the key gatekeeping of security common sense, with no communication or reporting friction. Minutes later the fix is delivered; the instant the fix is confirmed, the grouping dissolves, AI returns to the resource pool, and the expert goes back to his vacation. The entire organization's management cost survived only these few minutes during this crisis handling.

Everyday management and emergency hacks are two faces of the same orchestrator: internally, it forms temporary groupings by task; externally, it manages the boundary and triggers internal processes.

Three mechanisms support this operation, and they do not depend on any specific implementation:

**The grouping life-cycle protocol**. Event-driven grouping: members gather temporarily by event (task), dissolve immediately after the output is delivered, state information is frozen and sedimented, awaiting awakening by the next event. During a grouping's lifetime administrative overhead is zero—no standing reporting lines, weekly reports, or regular meetings, and it resets completely to zero when idle. The formation and dissolution of groupings is approved by humans, and the evolution of organizational structure (the recognition of the capability map, changes to workflow templates) is likewise computed by AI, but is gated outside the door of human confirmation in the form of a Diff audit report, with humans retaining a veto. This is consistent with the stance of single-writer control planes: the single writer of metadata (members, capabilities, structure) is the human, and AI only produces proposals.

**Fault recovery of suspended tasks**. Tasks within a grouping are given a TTL; after timeout the daemon does not blindly cancel, but queries back the terminal state from the counterpart or the disaster-recovery node—if unfinished, cancel and release resources; if finished, proceed to wrap up. This is the fault side of the event-driven mode: the event pushes the task to a person, and when the person loses contact the task must not be left suspended forever. This is precisely the mapping at the collaboration layer of the suspended-query mechanism of [Distributed Collaboration Topology §4.2](distributed-collaboration-topology-en.md#42-two-self-healing-patches): **the solution to suspension is to query back the terminal state, not to roll back automatically on timeout**.

**Transparent internally, encapsulated externally**. Inside a grouping it is a transparent mesh of high concurrency and seamless human-machine collaboration: data undergoes deterministic pre-validation by AI, human and machine run in parallel on the same work surface, saving level-by-level reporting and progress synchronization. Outside the organization it is an inscrutable static boundary—the liquid organization is an inward-facing symbiont with deep commercial secrets, not a borderless public plaza (for the protocol layer see [Distributed Collaboration Topology §7](distributed-collaboration-topology-en.md)). It trades an extremely low external trust overhead for the ultimate internal trust and efficiency.

### Internal Management: Workload Record and Contribution Index

The liquid organization's internal management needs no daily, weekly, or monthly reports—the orchestrator records every task flow, and workload is a by-product automatically sedimented, not a product of reporting. Instant settlement replaces periodic assessment: a task is paid the moment it is delivered, and the delay of incentive feedback shrinks from a month to a minute—this is a mechanism for unleashing potential; a delayed reward is not an incentive but a reckoning after the fact.

**Workload and contribution index are separate**. Workload (how many tasks done, how many lines of code, how many deliveries) is recorded directly, but it is only the base, not the evaluation. The contribution index is the weight of workload, measuring the quality and scarcity of output—directly encouraging quantity-gaming will only yield a pile-up of low-weight output, exactly what management wants to avoid. Guaranteed stable settlement follows workload; reputation and profit-sharing follow the contribution index; the two channels each govern one layer.

**The token scheme of the contribution index**. Consistent with the layering of the individual's positioning—the normal-distribution range is easy to quantify, the power-law tail relies on the market to discover prices—the contribution index has two sources by how quantifiable the output is:

- **System metrics (normal-distribution range)**: the objective indicators of deliverables are settled directly by AI—task completion quality (acceptance pass rate, rework rate), response time, collaboration load (number of tasks carried at the same time). This kind of contribution needs no human evaluation; the rules are open and transparent and cannot be tampered with after the fact. AI naturally takes on this metrologist role at zero cost—traditional organizations cannot achieve instant precise metering, which is why they degenerate into paying by time.
- **Peer evaluation (power-law tail)**: the quality of architecture decisions and product judgments cannot be systematically measured, so it is priced by stakeholder mutual evaluation—when a grouping dissolves, the collaborating parties vote on one another's judgment contributions, and a vote's value is weighted by the voter's contribution index (a high contributor's vote weighs more, suppressing collusive vote-pumping).

The two paths merge into the contribution index, which serves as the master gate for token issuance: when the contribution index rises, the corresponding tokens are issued; tokens can be exchanged for profit-sharing, priority task-selection rights, or governance voting rights. The root of anti-gaming mechanisms is not detection but structure—**the weight itself cannot be gamed**: rework deductions, settlement by result acceptance (rather than by hours worked), and power-law contributions priced by multi-party mutual evaluation rather than self-report mean that a quantity-gamer gets only a low-weight base, with nothing to gain.

### Software Form: SKILL-ized Operations, Platform-ized Capabilities, Graph-ized Memory

When the liquid organization lands on software, it is three interlocking things: **operations turned into SKILLs, SKILLs turned into a platform, and interaction with existing systems through SKILLs**.

**Operations turned into SKILLs**. A member's (human or AI Agent) operations on the system are no longer one-off verbal instructions or scattered scripts, but are sedimented as SKILLs—reusable, composable, verifiable units of operation. This upgrades "individual experience" into "organizational assets": the solution to a pitfall, an operation sequence of a business process, once written as a SKILL, can be reused by anyone (and any Agent), and experience no longer drains away as personnel move.

**SKILLs turned into a platform and a graph**. A SKILL is not a file in a static directory but networked/graph-ized—this is the essential difference between this architecture and a "script library." Core points (unfolded in [Graph Memory](graph-memory-en.md), [Stateless Agent Architecture](stateless-agent-architecture-en.md), [Aura Architecture](aura-architecture-en.md)):

- **Memory and skills share the same property graph**. Atomic facts (fixed-length 9-slot tuples) + a weight system form the base; a SKILL is not an independent entity but a "subgraph with a high tool index" in the graph—usage data automatically clusters and a SKILL boundary emerges, with no need to manually delimit "this counts as one skill." The boundary is emergent, drifts with use, and is naturally fluid and borderless.
- **The computation-timing spectrum determines the form**. Write-heavy (organizing/embedding/topology inference) trades for read-time determinism (direct loading, graph traversal); read-heavy (vector retrieval) trades for low write cost. Memory and the SKILL graph take the intermediate property form: build entity relations at write time, and at read time do deterministic traversal + vector-assisted discovery—the architecture-decision chain cannot be fuzzy-matched, and operational experience needs semantic discovery; the two take what they need at different ticks of the same spectrum.
- **The emergence loop**. Execution experience (pitfalls/solutions/operation sequences) → stored in the graph → clustering emerges a high-weight subgraph, i.e., a SKILL → runtime discovery (vector search + weight ranking) → execution → call counts written back to weights → affecting the next ranking. Once one instance's pitfall is solved and stored in the graph, the next execution anywhere immediately gets the new version—a zero-staleness window is the precondition of emergence.
- **Separation of execution and memory**. The memory system is the sole holder of session truth (append-only session + its own compression-point table), the Agent runtime is stateless (a turn is a pure function), and operation execution sinks into the containerized Effector—a SKILL's parsing and selection happen on the reasoning side, and the execution environment sees only the operation and its parameters. This separation lets the SKILL platform federate across organizations: the graph is the shared layer, the Effector is the local layer.

**Interaction with existing systems through SKILLs**. The liquid organization does not replace existing business systems; a SKILL is the adaptation layer through which the orchestrator reaches them—each external system integrated (ERP, ticketing, logistics) is one SKILL sedimented, rather than writing a one-off integration script for each system. Once SKILLs are platform-ized, "what the organization can do" equals "which SKILLs are in the graph," and the capability list is queryable, auditable, and composable—every internal process of the "business-process trigger line" in the main scenario is, at bottom, the execution of a SKILL. This also answers the metering question of the internal-management section: a SKILL's call records are naturally the raw data source of workload and contribution.

### Inter-Organizational Cooperation

The liquid organization is not an island; cooperation between organizations does not need to invent a new mechanism—two mature channels already exist:

- **Economic layer: monetary coordination**. Procurement, outsourcing, market transactions—inter-organization collaboration by price signals is the most mature part of a market economy, and the liquid organization merely lets smaller units (even one-person units) also participate in it as transaction subjects.
- **Software layer: federated platform**. When tighter collaboration than money is needed (shared identity, capability lists, task flow), carry it with a software platform that supports federation—such as the field model of [Aura Architecture](aura-architecture-en.md): each organizational node has equal status, independent metadata, and mutual recognition by a well-known protocol (for protocol details see [Distributed Collaboration Topology](distributed-collaboration-topology-en.md)). Only the special pricing problems beyond the economic layer (such as the collective pricing of non-quantifiable contributions) need a voting mechanism as a supplement, and that is the exception rather than the norm.

| Product stage | Suitable organizational form | Role combination |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Pre-PMF (exploration) | Liquid grouping / squad system | Prototyper + Builder + Sweeper |
| Growth | Squad system / liquid grouping (semi-fixed) | Builder + Sweeper + Grower |
| Maturity | Structured liquid organization / Teal organization (self-management sustains efficiency) | Sweeper + Grower + Maintainer |

The exploration stage needs flexibility and low coordination cost, and liquid grouping and the squad system fit best. The maturity stage needs stability and scale—but this is not a return to the control-oriented organization (whose applicable premises have weakened), but the liquid organization's own structuring: groupings turn from temporary to semi-fixed, experience sediments into SKILLs and processes, and stability is guaranteed by the orchestrator's mechanisms rather than by hierarchy. The organizational form should flow with the product stage, not be a one-time choice.

## The Individual: The Unit Within the Liquid Organization

### The True Cause of Position-Boundary Dissolution

Why can an individual become the basic unit of the liquid organization? The root cause lies in the dissolution of position boundaries.

Mainstream narrative: AI programming tools can now write both front-end and back-end code, so the front-end/back-end division boundary disappears. This attribution is shallow. The essence of the front-end/back-end division was never a technical boundary—an engineer can perfectly well understand both UI rendering and API design at the same time. The essence of the division is that **specialized division of labor lowers coordination cost**: when a person only does front-end, he does not need to repeatedly coordinate interface details with the back-end, and the communication channels shrink.

Conversely, the real driver of merging front-end and back-end is: **AI lowers the feasible cost of a single person covering multiple tech stacks**. Formerly, full-stack meant a jack of all trades and master of none (each skill at about 50% capacity); now AI patches the weak spots—a person specializes in one, and hands the rest to AI assistance; "one specialty, multiple abilities" turns from an ideal into an operable mode.

For details see the channel model in [Workplace Communication](workplace-communication-en.md): N decision-makers produce N(N-1)/2 communication channels; ten people means 45. Merging teams is essentially cutting the number of channels. Organizational restructuring happens when coordination cost falls—the organizational flattening that should have happened when the internet dividend period ended was accelerated by AI. Meituan merging its front-end and back-end teams and Ant turning testing into R&D have channel-cutting as their underlying logic—AI merely made this thing feasible.

### "Full-Stack" and the Layer Division Fail Together

Most people's first reaction in industry discussion is "the full-stack engineer is here." But "full-stack" is precisely a misunderstanding: "full-stack" = covering all layers; this meaning holds only when "layer" (front-end/back-end/database) is valid as a division axis. Once the layer division fails, "covering all layers" simultaneously loses its basis of definition—full-stack and front-end/back-end are concepts under the same paradigm; they are valid together or fail together.

The valid alternative axis is the five role archetypes proposed by Boris Cherny of the Claude Code team:

- **Prototyper**: proposes entirely new ideas, rapidly produces a large volume of ideas, most of which will not ship
- **Builder**: quickly turns prototypes into production-grade products or infrastructure
- **Sweeper**: polishes the UI, simplifies code and systems, retires unnecessary features, optimizes performance
- **Grower**: after the product is made, iterates continuously to improve PMF
- **Maintainer**: responsible for the long-term operation of mature systems, keeping them safe, reliable, and efficient

The value of this classification is not in its precision (it is far from industry consensus) but in that it **switched an axis**—from the skill axis (what you know) to the value axis (what you create). The two axes are orthogonal: a person can be "back-end stack + Builder," or "full-stack + Sweeper." The essence is a shift from "function-bound" to "stage-bound"; the reason a person spans multiple role types is that AI has flattened the cost of context-switching across roles.

The blind spot Boris did not answer is **cognitive switching cost**: prototype exploration needs divergent thinking, system maintenance needs convergent thinking, and the two modes conflict. AI lowered the information-switching cost (no need to re-understand the codebase) but did not lower the cost of cognitive-mode switching—not any two role types can be held by one person.

### The Super-Individual and the One-Person Company

"One-person company (OPC)" and "super-individual" are different layers: a one-person company is an organizational form (one person constitutes a complete delivery loop); a super-individual is productivity amplification (AI leverage + judgment). The basic unit of the liquid organization is defined by the super-individual—an autonomous small unit of one, two, or three people, positioning roles along the value axis, with AI filling the execution layer.

The liquid organization's boundary is fluid, and units can also have semi-fixed long-term cooperative relationships—a more fluid liquid organization. The OPC also cannot be entirely free of external cooperation: when connecting with a large platform, the large platform and the OPC themselves constitute a liquid organization.

The super-individual has a timeliness in its ecological niche, compared with the giant insects of the Carboniferous: the giant insects' flourishing relied on two temporary conditions—oxygen concentration high enough (atmospheric oxygen above 30%), and no birds yet in the sky. Corresponding to today: a considerable part of the "oxygen" the super-individual breathes comes from model vendors' subsidized monthly subscriptions; the top-tier $200 subscription buys compute equivalent to ten experts at a price far below human labor cost, and this subsidized pricing may not hold long-term; and the "bird"—the AI-native super-company that assembles the circulatory system (an orchestrator pumping context and tasks)—will sooner or later appear. Once the ecological niche is occupied, the selection pressure reverses: being big is no longer an advantage, being agile is (when oxygen rose again in the Cretaceous, insects did not grow back, because the age of birds had arrived). So the OPC is more likely a transitional niche than a final form: an individual going solo relies on the window where "diffusion efficiency is temporarily sufficient + the pumping system has not yet appeared"; within the window it is a rational choice for the super-individual to take the full acceleration dividend, and after the window closes, the organizational form that can be both large and fast will redefine the competitive conditions. This is two sides of the same coin with the two walls earlier—the walls explain why the dividend lands on the individual first, and the timeliness explains why it will not stay with the individual forever.

### The Individual's Positioning: The Power-Law Tail

AI's capability is normally distributed (see the three-layer limitations in [LLM Fundamentals](llm-fundamentals-en.md))—it performs well in "most cases" and is inevitably mediocre in the power-law tail (architecture decisions, product judgments, crisis handling). The engineering principle of the individual's positioning: **invest your energy where AI cannot do it**.

| Range | AI capability | Human positioning |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Normal-distribution range (writing code, format conversion, documents) | Efficient, low-cost | Should not compete, hand to AI |
| Power-law tail (architecture decisions, product judgments) | Inevitably mediocre | Core value zone |
| Egalitarian tasks (troubleshooting, validation) | A strength | Can collaborate |
| Anti-egalitarian tasks (exhaustive validation, innovative breakthroughs) | A weakness | Irreplaceable |

The subject-object inversion principle in [AI Programming Optimism Critique](ai-programming-optimism-critique-en.md): if a person cannot judge the quality of AI's output, the subject-object relation inverts—it is not you using AI, it is AI using you. The most valuable ability of an individual is not "how many tech stacks you know" but **judgment**: the ability to judge whether AI's output is right or wrong, to judge which problems are worth digging into deeply, and to judge the trade-offs of architectural choices. [AI Era Business Models](ai-era-business-models-en.md) has already pointed out: the value anchor moves from the deliverable to judgment—execution is free, judgment is charged, trust earns a premium.

### The Change in Assessment: From Production to Review

The shift of humans to oversight and review is the general trend of the AI era. The most informative signal is one interview case: in the third round of an interview for a junior full-stack position at SAP, the candidate was asked to **write prompts for a large model + do a code review of a full-stack application**—the assessment method has shifted from "writing code" to "reviewing the code AI produces."

This is a structural shift more fundamental than the merging of positions: the value on the production side is falling (AI can do it and is faster), the value on the review side is rising (AI does it poorly and it is structural), and the new boundary of the human-machine division is **AI produces, humans review**. Reviewing is easier than writing from scratch—local review (checking whether the passage AI gave is right) is not global understanding (understanding the entire codebase from zero); in the review scenario the human retains the judgment anchor, and no subject-object inversion occurs. "Code review" thus turns from a senior's extra duty into every engineer's primary duty—the change in assessment method defines the new capability threshold. The liquid organization institutionalizes this trend: the division-of-labor structure that runs through the earlier text—humans appear at the judgment points and oversight positions.

### Compound-Interest Thinking

An individual's competitiveness in the AI era does not come from the quality of a single output (AI is already good enough) but from **accumulation across sessions**. See [Agent Compound Interest](agent-compound-interest-en.md):

- **Memory**: correct once, effective forever—continuous calibration of judgment
- **Skills**: explore once, skip it next time—continuous optimization of the workflow
- **Judgment accumulation**: every time you review AI output you are training your own domain intuition

"Knowing how to write code" is no longer a moat—AI writes it faster. "Knowing how to judge" is—and judgment comes from continuously accumulated domain knowledge and review experience. People who pursue external variables (a stronger model) naturally do not understand compound interest, because the essence of compound interest is internal accumulation producing external results.

## Appendix: The Evolutionary Path of Organizational Forms

Organizational form is not a design choice but the solution to constraints. Once the constraints are understood, one can understand why each form appears, when it fails, and how AI changes the equation. Lining the forms up in the order in which constraints are lifted gives a path of evolution toward the liquid organization:

| Form | Constraint | Topology | How AI changes the equation |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Bureaucracy | One person effectively manages 6–7 people (Graicunas formula: the number of management relations grows as O(n²)) | Tree | AI expands an individual's management radius, no need for layers |
| Functional | An individual's skill breadth is limited | Tree (clustered by skill) | AI patches weak spots, one person can cover multiple stacks |
| Department | Dual constraint of skill + coordination | Tree (skill × region intersection) | Skill constraint loosened, coordination cost falls |
| Divisional | Cross-goal coordination cost too high, so each goes autonomous | Forest (multiple trees) | Coordination cost falls, boundaries can soften |
| Flattening | Middle management is an information relay, replaced by tools | Shallow tree | AI is a stronger information relay |
| Project-based | Person-project matching is the bottleneck, needs a platform to match | Temporary graph (edges created dynamically) | AI is a more advanced matching tool |
| Open organization | The trust problem—consensus mechanisms without employment relations | Dynamic network | AI makes the decision process transparent, partially substituting for trust |
| Liquid organization | The form after the above constraints are lifted simultaneously | Fluid network (temporary grouping + AI orchestration) | AI becomes the orchestrator itself |

Core rule: **the constraint is the lock, the tool is the key, and the organizational form is the shape after the lock is opened.** Bureaucracy's lock is management bandwidth, the functional organization's lock is the skill limitation, and the open organization's lock is trust. AI is not a new form but a new key—it opens several locks at once; the liquid organization is the form after multiple locks are opened together. The lifting of constraints is the enabling condition, and the driver comes from the productivity-structure shift at the start of the main text: the applicable premises of the control-oriented organization weaken, and the organization is forced to turn to the collaboration-oriented—the key and the driver converge in the liquid organization.

Bureaucracy is the baseline of this path, the organization of tree topology: one person can only effectively manage 6–7 people, so layers are inevitable. The tree gives "command chain + authority inheritance + deterministic order," at the cost of an expanding coordination channel (N(N-1)/2), difficult cross-departmental collaboration, and information distortion in the middle layers.

Experiments midway along the evolution also confirm the stubbornness of constraints: the squad system (Squads), Holacracy, and the Teal organization—the common bottleneck of these postmodern organizational experiments is coordination cost. It is not that the ideas are flawed but that they are too expensive to run: Holacracy "spends a great deal of time on circle governance," and the Teal organization depends on a "visionary leader" as a human coordinator and collapses when management changes. The smaller the basic unit, the more flexible, but the more coordination input is needed. The AI facilitator is an institutionalized coordination mechanism (organizing fragmented conversations, flagging conflicts, syncing consensus anchors, see the three-layer architecture in [Workplace Communication](workplace-communication-en.md)); removing the dependence on a human coordinator makes these "good ideas but too expensive" models operable—the liquid organization is the terminal form of this path.
